\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

  \caption{\textbf{Few-shot post-training collapses.}
  Closed-loop success rate of the mid-trained \base world action model after post-training on all demonstrations of each LIBERO suite (blue; 379--454 demonstrations, about 40 per task) and on three demonstrations per task (orange; 30 per suite), with the post-training recipe otherwise identical.
  The same recipe loses 16 to 49 points when demonstrations are scarce.
  Every bar is 50 trials on each of the 10 tasks of a suite.}
  \label{fig:teaser}

Robot learning is converging on a two-stage recipe.
A large model is first pre-trained on web-scale video and on cross-embodiment robot data, and is then \emph{post-trained} on demonstrations collected with the target robot in the target environment.
World action models (WAMs), which generate future video and robot actions jointly within one network~\cite{du2023unipi,wu2024gr1,zhu2025uwm,cen2025worldvla,cosmos3}, are the latest instance of this recipe: the video objective provides a dense, embodiment-agnostic training signal, and the action objective turns the same network into a policy.
When the post-training set is large, the recipe is remarkably effective.
On the four LIBERO suites~\cite{liu2023libero}, post-training the mid-trained \base WAM~\cite{cosmos3} on all available demonstrations reaches 87--99\% success (\cref{fig:teaser}, blue).

Demonstrations, however, are the most expensive ingredient of this pipeline.
Every new robot, workcell, or task requires a person to teleoperate it, and in many deployments only a handful of demonstrations per task is affordable.
We therefore ask how the standard recipe behaves when the post-training set is small, and define a \emph{few-shot post-training} protocol: three demonstrations per task, \ie, 30 demonstrations per LIBERO suite, with the post-training recipe otherwise unchanged.
The answer is sobering.
Success drops by 16 to 49 points (\cref{fig:teaser}, orange), to 58.0\% on LIBERO-10 and 48.8\% on LIBERO-Goal.
A world model that has been trained on millions of robot and human videos cannot turn 30 demonstrations into a reliable policy.

Why does so much prior knowledge help so little?
Post-training updates 1.4B parameters of the generator tower and the action heads on roughly 7.6k training windows, and the action head of the new embodiment is initialized from scratch.
More fundamentally, mid-training optimizes the weights for the \emph{joint} objective over a mixture of embodiments, and nothing in that objective asks the weights to be a good \emph{starting point} for a short fine-tune on an embodiment outside the mixture.
Being good at many embodiments and being quick to adapt to a new one are different properties.
This distinction is the premise of gradient-based meta-learning~\cite{finn2017maml,nichol2018reptile}, which so far has been applied to small policies on a few tasks of one robot rather than to billion-parameter world models.

We bring this idea to WAMs with a \method stage inserted between mid-training and post-training (\cref{fig:method}).
The stage uses no new data: it draws episodes from five robot datasets that are already part of the mid-training mixture, each recorded with a different embodiment.
In every episode it simulates the downstream problem, $K$ demonstrations of one source embodiment and $k$ steps of the exact post-training optimizer, loss, and trainable set, and then moves the weights a fraction of the way toward the adapted solution: the Reptile update~\cite{nichol2018reptile}.
Reptile is well matched to this scale.
Its update is first-order and needs neither a query set nor a second forward pass at the adapted point, so the model's own sharded parameters serve as the fast weights, the standard FSDP training stack~\cite{zhao2023fsdp} runs the inner loop unchanged, and the only extra state is one copy of the trainable parameters.
The resulting checkpoint $\thmeta$ is a drop-in replacement for the mid-trained weights, and we post-train from it with the unchanged recipe.

On the four LIBERO suites with three demonstrations per task, post-training from $\thmeta$ instead of the mid-trained weights raises the success rate from 58.0/48.8/72.0/82.8\% to 69.4/60.8/72.6/85.2\% and improves every one of the ten LIBERO-10 tasks.
The initialization also helps when data is plentiful: with all LIBERO-10 demonstrations the success rate rises from 87.2\% to 94.6\%.
In summary, our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item We identify and quantify the few-shot post-training problem of WAMs with a three-demonstrations-per-task protocol on the four LIBERO suites, on which the standard recipe loses 16--49 points.
  \item We propose \method, a Reptile-based stage between mid-training and post-training that optimizes the entire trainable set of the post-training recipe for few-shot adaptation, needs no additional data, and leaves the architecture and the post-training recipe unchanged.
  \item We show consistent gains on all four suites in the few-shot regime (+6.6 points on average, up to +12.0) and a +7.4 point gain with full data.
\end{itemize}
